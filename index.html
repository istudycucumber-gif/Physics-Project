01
Introduction to Electromagnetic Induction
How a changing magnetic field creates electricity — the discovery that lit up the world

Electromagnetic induction is the process by which a time-varying magnetic field produces an electromotive force (EMF) in a conductor. Discovered by Michael Faraday and Joseph Henry in 1831, it is the operating principle behind virtually every device that generates or transforms electrical energy — generators, transformers, motors, and wireless chargers alike.

The central concept is magnetic flux — the total magnetic field threading through a given area. Induction does not arise from the presence of a field, but from its change. A constant field, however strong, induces nothing. Motion, rotation, or varying current is what creates electricity.

Magnetic flux
Φ = B · A · cos θ
Φ = flux (Wb) | B = field (T) | A = area (m²) | θ = angle between B and normal to surface. Flux linkage with N turns = NΦ.

02
Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction
The quantitative laws that define when and how much EMF is induced

First Law: Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a closed circuit changes, an EMF is induced in the circuit. The induced EMF exists only while flux is changing.

Second Law: The magnitude of the induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of total flux linkage (NΦ) with time. Faster change → larger EMF. More turns → larger EMF.

Faraday's original experiment used a bar magnet moving in and out of a coil connected to a galvanometer. The needle deflected whenever the magnet moved but was still when it was stationary — proving it is the change in flux, not the flux itself, that causes induction.

Diagram 2 — Faraday's experiment: magnet moving into a coil
Faraday's second law — induced EMF
ε = −N · (dΦ/dt)
ε = induced EMF (V) | N = turns | dΦ/dt = rate of flux change (Wb/s). For finite changes: ε_avg = −N·(ΔΦ/Δt). The negative sign encodes Lenz's Law — opposition to change.
Advantages
  • Foundation of all electrical power generation
  • Elegant single equation covers every geometry
  • Scales from nano-sensors to gigawatt generators
  • Universally applicable to any conductor shape
Limitations
  • Requires continuous mechanical input
  • Energy losses due to resistance in real coils
  • Breaks down at relativistic speeds
  • Does not specify direction of induced current

03
Lenz's Law
Nature always opposes the change — conservation of energy expressed electromagnetically

Lenz's Law (Heinrich Lenz, 1834) states: the induced current always flows in a direction such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux that caused it. It is the physical meaning of the negative sign in Faraday's equation and is a direct consequence of conservation of energy.

When a magnet approaches a coil, the induced current creates a field that repels the magnet — you must do work to push it closer. That work becomes electrical energy in the coil. Nature never gives energy for free.

Diagram 3 — Lenz's law: approach and withdrawal of a magnet
Lenz's law — direction of induced EMF
ε = −dΦ/dt
The negative sign means: if flux increases → induced EMF opposes the increase. If flux decreases → induced EMF tries to maintain it. This is conservation of energy in electromagnetic form.
Significance
  • Confirms conservation of energy
  • Determines current direction in circuits
  • Basis of magnetic braking systems
  • Explains back-EMF in motors
Drawbacks
  • Always creates opposing force — energy must overcome it
  • Reduces motor efficiency (back-EMF)
  • Voltage spikes when current is switched off

04
Eddy Currents (Foucault Currents)
Circular induced currents in bulk conductors — simultaneously a nuisance and a powerful tool

When a solid conductor is placed in a changing magnetic field, EMF is induced throughout its bulk — not along a single wire. These internal EMFs drive closed loops of current that swirl like eddies in water (hence the name). They dissipate energy as Joule heat (I²R), which is both their main problem and their main application.

To reduce eddy current losses in transformer cores, the core is built from thin laminated sheets insulated from each other — restricting the eddy current loops to tiny paths and slashing the power loss dramatically.

Diagram 4 — Eddy currents in a solid plate vs. laminated core
Power dissipated by eddy currents
P = k · B² · f² · t² · V
k = material constant | B = flux density (T) | f = frequency (Hz) | t = lamination thickness (m) | V = volume (m³). Halving thickness → losses drop to ¼. High frequency → ferrite cores required.
Useful applications
  • Induction heating: cooktops, metal hardening
  • Electromagnetic damping in instruments
  • Eddy-current brakes in trains and rides
  • Non-destructive testing of metal components
Harmful effects
  • Energy loss as heat in motors and transformers
  • Overheating reduces core lifespan
  • Requires expensive laminated construction
  • Skin effect limits capacity at high frequency

05
Self-Induction
A coil opposing changes in its own current — electromagnetic inertia

When the current through a coil changes, the flux it produces also changes, inducing an EMF in the coil itself that opposes the change. This is self-induction. The property is quantified by the self-inductance L (Henry). An inductor is the electromagnetic equivalent of mechanical inertia — it resists any change in current, storing energy in its magnetic field (U = ½LI²) and releasing it back when current decreases.

In an LR circuit, current does not jump instantly to its final value — it grows exponentially with time constant τ = L/R, a direct consequence of the back-EMF opposing the change.

Diagram 5 — Self-induction: solenoid cross-section and LR circuit
Self-inductance, back-EMF and energy stored
Φ = LI → ε = −L(dI/dt) → U = ½LI²
L = self-inductance (H) | I = current (A) | ε = back-EMF (V) | U = energy stored (J). For a solenoid: L = μ₀N²A/l. Time constant τ = L/R governs current rise/fall.
Advantages
  • Smooths current in power circuits (choke)
  • Stores energy in the magnetic field
  • Essential in LC oscillators and radio tuning
  • Protects circuits from sudden current spikes
Drawbacks
  • Back-EMF spikes can destroy transistors
  • Physically bulky at low frequencies
  • I²R losses in coil resistance
  • Iron cores saturate at high currents

06
Mutual Induction
Two coils sharing magnetic energy — the principle that makes transformers possible

Mutual induction is the phenomenon where a changing current in one coil (primary) induces an EMF in a nearby second coil (secondary) via their shared magnetic flux. No electrical connection is needed — energy crosses entirely through the changing magnetic field.

The ideal transformer is the direct engineering application. By choosing the turns ratio N₂/N₁, any voltage can be stepped up or down. India's power grid uses 765 kV transmission — stepped up from ~11 kV at generators — to reduce I²R losses in cables over hundreds of kilometres, then stepped back down to 230 V at homes, all via mutual induction.

Diagram 6 — Transformer cross-section (mutual induction)
Mutual inductance and transformer equations
ε₂ = −M·(dI₁/dt) | V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁ = I₁/I₂
M = mutual inductance (H) | M = k·√(L₁·L₂), k = coupling coefficient (0–1) | Ideal transformer: power in = power out (V₁I₁ = V₂I₂). Real efficiency: 95–99%.
Advantages
  • Enables long-distance power transmission
  • No electrical contact — galvanic isolation
  • Foundation of wireless power and NFC
  • Very high efficiency (95–99%) in transformers
Limitations
  • Works only with AC — cannot transform DC
  • Leakage flux reduces coupling efficiency
  • Core losses (hysteresis + eddy currents)
  • Wireless systems significantly less efficient

Conclusion

Electromagnetic induction is not merely a chapter in a textbook — it is the invisible thread that holds modern civilisation together. Faraday's Laws give us the quantitative foundation. Lenz's Law provides direction and connects induction to conservation of energy. Eddy currents show induction in bulk matter — harmful in transformer cores, invaluable in induction cooktops. Self-induction reveals the electromagnetic flywheel. Mutual induction enables transformers, wireless power, and the entire electrical grid.

All six concepts reduce to one equation: ε = −dΦ/dt. A single negative sign encodes a deep truth — that electricity is never free, and that changing magnetism creates it. That elegance is perhaps the most inspiring lesson this chapter offers.
